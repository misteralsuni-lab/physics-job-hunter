### KEY VOCABULARY (on board at start)

| Term | Simple Meaning |
|------|----------------|
| **Alpha (α)** | Particle: 2p + 2n. Stopped by paper. Most ionising. |
| **Beta (β)** | Particle: fast electron. Stopped by aluminium. Moderately ionising. |
| **Gamma (γ)** | Electromagnetic wave. Stopped by thick lead. Least ionising. |
| **Half-life** | Time taken for half the radioactive nuclei in a sample to decay |
| **Activity** | The rate at which a radioactive source decays (measured in Bq) |
| **Count-rate** | The number of decay events detected per unit time (Bq) |

### LESSON STRUCTURE

| Stage | Time | Activity | Purpose |
|-------|------|----------|---------|
| **Starter** | 0–3 min | **Inquiry-led hook:** Image of a glowing watch dial on screen. "Would you wear a watch that glowed in the dark without batteries?" Take 2–3 brief responses. Then: "Over 100 years ago, workers painted these watch dials using a radioactive substance called radium. They believed it was harmless. Many later became seriously ill." Key question: "How could something invisible cause such serious harm?" Transition immediately into: "Today we will answer that question — starting with the three types of radiation emitted by radioactive materials." | Activate curiosity; ground abstract physics in real human story; establish the "why" before the "what" |
| **Introduction** | 3–8 min | **Teacher-led explanation with diagrams:** Draw three types of emission side-by-side on the board/Slides. Use colour coding: α (red, slow, heavy), β (blue, faster, lighter), γ (purple, wave). State: "Alpha and beta are particles. Gamma is a wave — that's why it takes more to stop it." Link to real-world: "Why is alpha the most ionising? Because its charge is +2, so it interacts most strongly with atoms." Pause after each type for a **cold-call check:** "Ali, what's the range of alpha in air?" (5 cm). | Core conceptual input |
| **Main Activity 1** | 8–14 min | **Half-life concept build:** Show a decay curve (pre-plotted on screen). Guide students through reading it step-by-step. "Start at 0 decay events per second. Move along the time axis until count-rate halves. How long?" (2 min). Label this on the graph. Repeat for the second half-life. Identify: the time is the same each time — this is half-life. Then **mini-practical modelling:** "Imagine we have 80 dice. Each die that shows a 1 'decays' — we remove it. Roll, remove, count the survivors. What do you predict the graph will look like?" Show a 3-roll animation or table of simulated dice data. | Core concept + kinesthetic modelling; addresses the misconception that decay is linear |
| **Main Activity 2** | 14–18 min | **Calculation practice — think-write-share:** Present two questions on screen. Students work in silence for 3 min, then share with their partner. **Question 1 (foundation):** A sample has activity 800 Bq. Its half-life is 6 hours. What is the activity after 24 hours? *Ans: 50 Bq (800 → 400 → 200 → 100 → 50)*. **Question 2 (stretch):** A sample has half-life of 8 minutes. After 40 minutes, the count-rate is 15 counts per minute. What was the initial count rate? *Ans: 480 cpm (work backwards: 15 → 30 → 60 → 120 → 240 → 480)*. **Teacher walk-through of Q2 on the board** after students attempt it. **Differentiation:** Foundation — provide a blank half-life table; Stretch — ask students to calculate in fractions (e.g. "what fraction remains after 5 half-lives?" = 1/32). | Numeracy within physics — higher-order calculation and peer assessment |
| **Plenary** | 18–20 min | **Exit ticket:** Show an application question: "A hospital uses a radioactive tracer to scan a patient's thyroid. The tracer has a half-life of 6 hours. Explain why a short half-life is important." Students write on a sticky note (or type in chat) and submit. Read 1-2 aloud to class as you collect. Preview next lesson: "Tomorrow: we look at how background radiation affects real measurements, and the practical uses vs dangers of each type." | Summary + transition to next lesson (formative assessment capture) |

### SAFEGUARDING & HEALTH & SAFETY NOTE

- **No radioactive sources are used in this lesson.**
- The dice-rolling activity is a simulation only; no laboratory safety protocols required.
- If a student raises a question about nuclear weapons or disasters (Chernobyl, Hiroshima): acknowledge the question briefly, redirect to the physics ("That's a different type of reaction — fission, not decay — and we cover it in Unit 4 Topic 8"), and note any student who appears distressed for follow-up with the school's Designated Safeguarding Lead (DSL).
